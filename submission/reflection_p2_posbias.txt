I tested the judge by evaluating each pair twice, swapping the order of A and B, and mapping the second verdict back to the original labels. If both orders agreed, I kept the verdict; if they disagreed, I marked it as a TIE and flagged it. In the baseline test, 2 out of 22 pairs disagreed, including 1 hard flip where the winner completely reversed. In the real A/B test suite, 6 out of 14 pairs disagreed. This showed me that the judge can be sensitive to the position of an answer rather than just its content, meaning single-order results may be unreliable. The trade-off is that checking both orders doubles the number of judge calls and can produce more TIEs, but it makes the evaluation more robust to position bias. One limitation is that this check cannot catch a judge that consistently gives the wrong verdict in both orders.
